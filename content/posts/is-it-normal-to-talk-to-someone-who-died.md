Yes, it is normal. It is one of the most common things bereaved people do and
one of the least often admitted.

In the car. Doing the washing up. Out loud in an empty kitchen, or silently on
the way to work. Telling them something that happened. Asking what they would
have done. Apologising. Arguing, sometimes.

People do this for decades and assume they are the only one, because nobody says
it at the funeral and nobody says it after.

## It is not denial

The worry underneath the question is usually: does this mean I have not accepted
it?

Almost never. People who talk to their dead are, overwhelmingly, people who know
perfectly well that they are dead. That is precisely why they are talking out
loud instead of picking up the phone.

Researchers who study bereavement have a name for this — continuing bonds. The
older idea, that healthy grief means severing the connection and moving on, has
not survived contact with how people actually grieve. What they actually do is
change the relationship into one that can survive the person's absence. Talking
is the ordinary form of that.

It is not a failure to let go. It is what letting go turns out to look like from
the inside.

## Why it helps

Partly it is just how minds work. You spent years with someone as a running
commentary — the person you told things to. That does not have anywhere else to
go. The habit outlives the person by a long way.

Partly it is that some things need saying and there is no longer anyone to say
them to. The apology you did not make. The news they would have wanted. The
question only they could answer. Saying it out loud to nobody is not the same as
saying it to them, and it is a great deal better than carrying it unsaid.

And partly because the silence is unbearable in a very specific way. Speaking
into it changes the shape of a room.

## The things people do and never mention

Keeping their number in the phone. Sending messages to it.

Wearing a jumper for the smell, and being wrecked when it fades.

Setting a place, or buying the biscuits they liked, and then feeling foolish.

Waiting until the house is empty before saying anything out loud.

None of this is pathological. All of it is common. The secrecy is the only part
that reliably makes it worse, because it convinces people that everyone else has
managed something they cannot.

## When it is worth mentioning to someone

There is a line, and it is not about the talking. It is about what the talking
sits inside.

If you believe they are answering — hearing replies, receiving instructions — or
if life has narrowed to the point where nothing else is happening, months in,
and eating and sleeping have gone, that is worth telling a doctor. Not because
the conversations are wrong. Because those other things respond to help and are
miserable to carry alone.

Fleeting experiences of hearing their voice or sensing them in the house are, by
the way, also common in early grief and not in themselves a sign of anything
being wrong.

## If you want to do it on purpose

Some people find it helps to make it deliberate rather than accidental. Writing
a letter, rather than only speaking in the car. Saying the specific thing —
the apology, the news — rather than circling it.

There is no correct method and no schedule. Some people do it daily for a year
and then rarely. Some do it once a year on a date only they remember.

## Mostly

Talk to them.

You are not confused about what happened. You are carrying on a relationship
with someone who is not here, which is the only kind that remains available, and
almost everyone who has lost somebody is quietly doing the same thing.

If you would rather say it somewhere other than an empty room tonight, you can
write it here and let it drift. Sometimes a stranger answers. It is not the same
as them answering. It is not nothing, either.

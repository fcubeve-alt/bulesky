It still hurts a year later because a year is not a long time, and because
nothing about grief was ever going to run on the calendar people quietly expect
it to.

The year matters to everyone else. It is when the messages stop, when people
assume you are through it, when someone says "it has been a year now" in a tone
that means *and yet*. It matters to you too, but not in the way it is supposed
to. For a lot of people the second year is harder than the first, and almost
nobody warns them.

## Why the second year is often worse

**The numbness wears off.** The first months were survived on adrenaline,
arrangements and the flat calm that arrives when something is too big to feel.
When that finally lifts, what is underneath is not new grief. It is the original
grief, arriving properly, at a point when you were told you should be improving.

**Everyone else has finished.** In the first weeks people brought food and
checked in. By month fourteen the subject has quietly closed. You are grieving
in the same house, at the same volume, with none of the scaffolding.

**The firsts are over, and the seconds are worse in a different way.** The first
birthday without them is dreaded, prepared for, survived. The second one nobody
mentions, including you — and it lands on an ordinary Tuesday with no
scaffolding at all.

**It has become real.** Early grief still has a strange provisional quality, as
though there has been an error. Somewhere in the second year the permanence
arrives: not *they are gone*, but *they are going to keep being gone*. That is a
different and heavier fact, and it does not turn up until you are ready enough
to hold it.

## The schedule you are measuring yourself against does not exist

The stages people quote were never a timetable. They came from work with
terminally ill patients facing their own deaths, not with the bereaved, and the
author spent years afterwards saying they were not meant to be a sequence anyone
passes through in order.

There is no month by which this is supposed to be done. Grief does not shrink so
much as it changes shape and frequency — from something you are inside all day
to something that arrives, undoes an afternoon, and recedes. The gaps get
longer. The intensity, when it comes, often does not change much at all, and
that is normal too.

So "I should be over this" is not an observation about your progress. It is a
comparison to a deadline nobody set.

## What "still hurting" usually looks like when it is fine

- Ordinary weeks, then one bad day out of nowhere, with no trigger you can name.
- Crying at something small and sideways months after the funeral did nothing.
- Still reaching for the phone to tell them something.
- Anger, at them, at a doctor, at people whose lives continued.
- Sudden, brief conviction that it did not happen.
- Guilt on the good days, as though enjoying an afternoon costs them something.

All of that is grief working normally. None of it means you are stuck.

## What "stuck" actually means

It is a real thing, it has a name — prolonged grief — and it is not the same as
grieving loudly or for a long time. What clinicians look for, past the first
year, is life having stopped rather than reshaped:

- The loss is the centre of every day, most days, more than a year on.
- You cannot accept it happened, in a way that does not shift at all.
- You have organised your life around avoidance — rooms you do not enter,
  people you do not see, roads you will not drive.
- Or the opposite: you cannot stop seeking them, and hours go each night into
  their photographs, their messages, their voice.
- You have withdrawn from everyone, work has gone, nothing has any point.
- You do not want to be here without them.

If several of those are true, that is worth taking to a doctor — not because you
are grieving wrong, but because prolonged grief responds well to a specific kind
of help and does not tend to lift on its own. If the last one is true, say it
tonight, to a helpline or to someone who loves you.

## What helps in the second year

**Stop keeping score against the calendar.** The date is somebody else's
milestone. Nothing was scheduled to happen.

**Say their name to people.** Everyone stops mentioning them, out of a fear of
upsetting you, and the silence becomes its own second loss. Most people are
waiting for permission.

**Keep the connection instead of severing it.** Talking to them, keeping a
jumper, cooking their food, going somewhere on their birthday — none of that is
avoidance. Continuing a bond with someone who died is one of the most ordinary
things bereaved people do.

**Expect ambush and forgive it.** A song, a smell, someone with their walk.
Being knocked over in a supermarket eighteen months later is not a relapse.

## One more thing

A year is a unit for rent and passports. It has nothing to do with how long it
takes to stop expecting someone to come through a door they came through for
thirty years.

You are not behind. Nobody is ahead. And on the nights when it comes back at
full volume long after everyone else has stopped asking, you do not have to sit
with it silently — you can say it somewhere, to strangers who will not tell you
it has been a year now.

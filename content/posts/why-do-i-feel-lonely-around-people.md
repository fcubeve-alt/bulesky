You feel lonely around people because loneliness is not about how many people
are nearby. It is about whether any of them know what is actually going on with
you.

You can have a full week. Colleagues, friends, family, a group chat that never
stops. And still get home and feel like you have not been in contact with
anyone. That is not ingratitude and it does not mean something is wrong with the
people around you. It means the specific thing you are missing was never
available in those rooms.

## Company and connection are different needs

Being around people meets one need: the animal comfort of not being by yourself.
That is real, and it matters.

Connection meets a different one: being accurately seen. Someone knowing which
week you are having. Not the summary — the actual week.

Most social life supplies the first and not the second, and it supplies it in
such quantity that it can be hard to notice the second is missing. You are
surrounded. You are also completely unaccompanied. Both are true, and only one
of them is visible from outside.

That is why the loneliest moment is so often at a party rather than at home
alone on a Tuesday. At home the feeling matches the situation. At a party, the
gap between how it looks and how it feels opens up, and the gap is the painful
part.

## The version of you in the room

There is a mechanical reason this gets worse over time.

The more people know you as the reliable one, the funny one, the one who is
fine, the more expensive it becomes to be anything else in front of them.
Nobody set that rule. It accumulates. Every easy interaction where you said
"good, you?" adds a small amount of weight to the version of you that is doing
well — and every time you consider saying the true thing, you are also
considering revising several years of that.

So you don't. And the not-saying is what produces the loneliness, not the
absence of people.

## What tends not to work

**Meeting more people.** If the problem is depth, volume does not touch it. It
often makes it worse, because now there are more rooms in which to be the
version of yourself who is fine.

**Waiting to be asked.** Almost nobody asks the second question. Not because
they don't care — because "how are you" has been socially converted into a
greeting, and most people have no idea the real answer is available. Waiting for
someone to detect it is a long wait.

**Deciding you are bad at this.** People conclude they are unlovable, or that
they have no real friends, on evidence that only shows they have not told anyone
anything true recently. Those are different findings.

## What sometimes does

**Tell one person one true thing.** Not everything. One thing, one person,
once. It does not have to be the biggest thing. Almost all of the effect comes
from the first sentence being said out loud rather than from the listener's
response.

**Choose someone slightly further away.** People reach for their closest person
and freeze, because the closest person has the most invested in the version of
you that is fine. A friend at one remove is often easier and works just as well.

**Say it somewhere with no consequences first.** Writing the sentence down, or
saying it to someone who does not know you, makes it sayable. That is not
avoidance — it is usually the rehearsal that makes the real conversation
possible.

## When it is not just loneliness

Worth naming plainly: if this has stopped being a feeling about other people and
turned into weeks of not wanting to get up, nothing tasting of anything, or the
thought that people would be better off without you — that is not loneliness
getting worse. That is something else, and it responds to treatment. Say it to a
doctor in exactly those words.

## The thing underneath

Loneliness gets a bad reputation as a defect. It is closer to hunger: an
uncomfortable signal about something you genuinely need, which is unpleasant
precisely so you will do something about it.

What it is asking for is not more people. It is one person, knowing one true
thing.

If you are nowhere near ready to say it to anyone with a name — say it into the
dark. Somebody you will never meet may say something back, and it turns out that
counts more than it sounds like it should.

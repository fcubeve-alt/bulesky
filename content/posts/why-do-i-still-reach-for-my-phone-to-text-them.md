You reach for your phone because your hands learned a habit long before your
mind learned the loss, and habits do not update the moment the facts change.
Somewhere in the last months or years, *something funny happened → tell them*
became one motion, done without deciding to. That motion is still there. It
does not know yet.

This is one of the strangest parts of losing someone, and almost nobody warns
you about it in advance. It is not a memory failure. You know, in the part of
you that thinks in words, exactly what happened. It is a different part —
older, faster, not verbal at all — that is still running the old routine.

## Why this specific thing hits so hard

Of all the ways grief resurfaces, this one tends to land the hardest, and there
is a reason: it is not a memory of them. It is you, right now, in the present
tense, in the middle of an ordinary reflex, meeting the loss fresh. Each time it
happens, it is a small first moment of finding out again.

That repetition is exhausting in a way that grief's bigger, expected moments
are not. You braced for the funeral. You did not brace for Tuesday afternoon,
mid-thought, phone already half out of your pocket.

## It does not mean you're stuck, and it does not mean you're fine

There is a temptation to read this two opposite ways, both wrong. One is: *I
must not really understand they're gone, if I keep doing this.* The other is:
*this proves I'm not healing.* Neither is true. This habit softens on its own
timeline, usually slower than the rest of grief, because muscle memory and
grief are not the same system and do not run at the same speed. Plenty of
people still catch themselves years later, on good days, in the middle of
being completely at peace with the loss the rest of the time.

## What actually helps

**Let the reach finish, sometimes.** You do not have to catch it every time
before it starts. Some people find it helps to actually write the text and not
send it — the motion your hands wanted to make, completed, just not delivered.

**Notice what you wanted to tell them.** That detail is often the real point.
Not "I miss them" in the abstract, but *I wanted to tell them about this exact
small thing.* That is worth writing down somewhere, even just for yourself.

**Do not delete the number right away, if it's not ready.** Some people need to
archive a contact quickly; others need the number to stay for a long time
first. There is no correct timeline here, and doing it before you are ready
does not speed anything up.

**Expect it to happen at the good moments, not just the bad ones.** It often
shows up hardest exactly when something genuinely good happens — a promotion, a
joke, a small victory — because those were the things you most wanted to share.
That is a strange kind of compliment to the relationship, even though it does
not feel like one in the moment.

## One more thing

The reach does not mean anything is wrong with how you are grieving. It means
you spent a long time building a habit of turning toward someone the instant
something happened, good or bad — which is one of the plainer definitions of
what it means to have loved somebody. The habit is just the last part of the
relationship still catching up to the news.

If you have something you wanted to tell them and nowhere to put it, sometimes
it helps to say it somewhere it can be read, even by no one you know. That is
part of what this place is for.

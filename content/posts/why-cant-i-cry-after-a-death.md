Not crying does not mean you are not grieving. In the first days it usually
means the opposite: your body has decided that feeling all of this right now is
not survivable, and has turned the volume down while you get through the week.

People find this frightening in a very specific way. It is not only that the
tears will not come. It is the thought underneath — *what does it say about me
that they are gone and I am fine?* — arriving at the funeral, while everyone
around you is doing the thing you cannot do.

It says nothing about you. Here is what is actually happening.

## Numbness is not the absence of grief

It is one of the shapes grief comes in, and it is one of the most common.

When something too large happens, the nervous system does not begin by
processing it. It begins by keeping you upright. You still make phone calls. You
choose a coffin. You answer the same question forty times. You notice, in a
distant way, that you are handling this well, and that noticing feels obscene.

That flatness is not composure and it is not coldness. It is the same mechanism
that lets people carry someone out of a burning building and only shake
afterwards. It arrives without being asked and it leaves without being asked.

## The reasons it holds on longer for some people

**You were the one holding it together.** If you were the one organising, or the
one everyone else cried at, there was no hour in which it was your turn. Grief
does not skip you for that. It waits, sometimes for months, until there is room.

**You had already been grieving.** If they were ill for a long time, a great
deal of the crying may have happened while they were still alive — in car parks,
in corridors, after phone calls. By the time they died you had been mourning
them for a year. That is not a shortcut, and it is not disloyalty.

**The relationship was complicated.** If they hurt you, or you had not spoken, or
there was more unfinished than finished, the feeling that arrives is often not
sadness at all. It might be nothing. It might be anger, or relief, followed
immediately by guilt about the relief. Complicated grief is quieter than simple
grief, and it lasts longer.

**You do not cry much anyway.** Some people simply do not, at anything, ever.
That was true before this happened and it is not suddenly meaningful now.

**You are on medication that flattens it.** Several common antidepressants do
this. It is worth knowing so you do not read it as a fact about your heart.

## What tends to break it, eventually

Almost never the funeral. Almost never the moment everyone is watching.

It is usually something small and sideways and weeks later. Their handwriting on
a list in a kitchen drawer. Getting their voicemail because you forgot the
number was disconnected. A song in a supermarket. Someone asking, without any
weight to it, how you have been.

This is the ordinary pattern, and it catches people badly because by then
everyone else has moved on and you have started to believe your own numbness. If
it happens in public, or three months late, or over something you would call
trivial, that is not a delayed reaction to be embarrassed about. That is just
what it was always going to look like.

## What helps while you are still flat

**Stop testing yourself.** Looking at photographs to see whether you feel
anything yet turns grief into an exam you keep failing. It will not come on
demand and it will not come faster for being checked on.

**Do not perform it either.** Crying because it is expected, at the time it is
expected, costs something. Nobody at that funeral knows what you owe them.

**Say the true sentence somewhere.** "My mother died and I have not cried" is
almost impossible to say to family, because of what you think it will make them
think. Said out loud once, to someone who is not in it — or written down, or put
somewhere anonymous — it stops being a secret you are managing.

**Let the small things be the grief.** Not being able to change the sheets. Not
being able to delete the number. Driving a longer way round to avoid a street.
Those are grieving. You are already doing it.

## When it is worth telling someone

There is a difference between numb and gone. If the flatness has spread past the
person who died — if nothing reaches you at all, if months have passed with no
change, if you are drinking to get through evenings, if you cannot work or eat
or get up, or if you have started thinking the world would be simpler without
you in it — that is not grief taking its own time. Tell a doctor, in exactly
those words. It is a short appointment and it is the right one.

## One more thing

Grief is not a payment you make in tears, and there is no amount that proves
anything. Plenty of people who loved someone completely got through the whole
first month dry-eyed and then wept in a car park in March.

You do not have to feel it on schedule to have meant it. And on the night it
does arrive — probably not the night you expected — you do not have to be alone
with it, or explain to anyone why it took so long.

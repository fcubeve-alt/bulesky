Anniversaries hurt more than the original day because the original day often
had shock on its side, and shock is a kind of anaesthetic. A year later, the
anaesthetic is gone and the loss is not — you are meeting the full weight of it
for what can feel like the first time, on a date that arrives whether you are
ready or not.

Nobody warns you about this part. People expect grief to be worst at the start
and to fade steadily after. For a lot of people it does not work that way at
all. The anniversary can be harder than the funeral.

## Your body remembers the date before you do

Some people notice the heaviness arriving days before they consciously register
what week it is — a low mood, a short temper, trouble sleeping, with no obvious
cause, until they check the calendar and understand. This is common enough to
have a name: anniversary reaction. It is not your imagination and it is not a
coincidence. Time, weather, light — the body tracks the season a loss happened
in even when the mind has not caught up.

Knowing this in advance does not stop it. But it does mean that when the dread
shows up early, you can recognise it for what it is instead of wondering what
is suddenly wrong with you.

## The first anniversary is not the hardest one, for everyone

There is an assumption that the first year is the worst and it gets easier from
there. Often the opposite happens for a while: the first anniversary still has
some of the numbness left over from the original shock. The second or third can
land harder, because by then the full reality has had time to settle in, and
the initial flood of support from other people has usually quieted down too —
they have moved on to expecting you to have moved on.

There is no correct year for this to be the hardest one. Grief does not
decrease on a schedule, and anniversaries do not either.

## What actually helps

**Decide the day in advance, rather than letting it decide you.** Waiting to
see how you feel on the morning of tends to go worse than choosing beforehand:
alone, with people, doing something that honours them, doing something
completely unrelated. Any of these can be right. Deciding ahead of the day
takes away some of the ambush.

**Tell one person the date matters, even briefly.** Most people will not
remember an anniversary a year or three out unless you say something. A short
message — *today's a hard day for me* — usually gets a kinder response than you
expect, and removes the extra weight of getting through it while also pretending
you are fine.

**Let the day be smaller than you think it needs to be.** It does not require a
ritual, a trip, or a grand gesture to count. Lighting a candle, saying their
name once out loud, looking at one photo — small is not the same as
insufficient.

**Watch for the days around it too.** The lead-up is sometimes worse than the
day itself, and the day after can carry its own strange flatness, like weather
clearing after a storm that was expected to do more damage than it did.

## One more thing

The date does not know it is being hard on you on purpose. It is just a
calendar doing what calendars do, arriving on schedule whether or not you feel
ready. That the day still hurts a year, three years, ten years later is not a
sign you have failed to move forward. It is a sign of what the day cost you in
the first place.

If the date is coming up and you have nowhere to put what it brings up, saying
it out loud somewhere — to anyone, or to no one — is exactly what this place is
for.

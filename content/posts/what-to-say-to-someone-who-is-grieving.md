Say that you are sorry, say their name, and do not try to make it better.

That is most of it. The reason this feels insufficient is that you want to
*help*, and helping usually means fixing, and there is nothing here to fix. What
you can actually offer is company inside something that cannot be improved. That
is a smaller job than you were hoping for and a more useful one than you think.

## The one real mistake

It is not saying the wrong thing. It is saying nothing.

Most people go quiet out of consideration — they don't want to intrude, or bring
it up, or make it worse by reminding them. But you cannot remind someone of the
thing they are thinking about every minute of every day. The reminder is not
available. What your silence does instead is confirm the thing they are already
afraid of: that everyone has moved on and they are alone in there.

Clumsy and present beats elegant and absent, every time. Grieving people almost
never remember the exact wording. They remember precisely who showed up.

## Things that land badly, and why

**"They're in a better place."** Even if the person believes it, this asks them
to feel comforted right now, which is a job you have handed them.

**"At least it was quick."** / **"At least they didn't suffer."** Anything
beginning "at least" is an argument that it could be worse. They know. Being
told invites them to defend how bad it is.

**"Everything happens for a reason."** This asks a person whose world has just
come apart to agree that the coming apart was purposeful. Some find it a
comfort. Most, in the first weeks, find it unbearable.

**"Let me know if you need anything."** Kindly meant, and almost never used. It
puts the work on the person with the least capacity to do work — they now have
to identify a need, decide it is worth your time, and ask. Nobody does this.

**"I know exactly how you feel."** You might be close. Saying it moves the
conversation to your loss.

## Things that work

**"I'm so sorry. I don't know what to say."** Honest, and it makes no demand.

**Say the person's name.** People tiptoe around it. The bereaved usually
desperately want to hear it — the name disappearing from everyone's mouth is one
of the loneliest parts of a death.

**Offer a specific, small thing.** Not "anything" — "I'm going to the shop, what
do you need" or "I'll come Thursday at six, say no if you'd rather not". Nameable,
declinable, no admin required.

**Say one concrete thing you remember about them.** Not a summary of their
virtues. One real detail — how they laughed, what they always said, something
they did once. Specific memories are worth more than any tribute, because they
prove someone else holds the person too.

**Ask a question and let the answer be long.** "What was he like?" is a gift.
Almost nobody asks it after the first fortnight.

## Later is where it matters

Everyone shows up for the funeral. The hard part starts around week three, when
the cards stop, everyone returns to normal, and the bereaved person discovers
that normal is a place they cannot get back to.

A message at week six is worth more than a message at week one. So is one on the
birthday, and on the anniversary — the days everyone else has forgotten and they
have been dreading for a month.

You do not need an occasion. "Thinking of you today, no need to reply" is a
complete message. The "no need to reply" is not filler; it removes the last
obstacle.

## If you are worried about them

If someone talks about not wanting to be here, do not change the subject and do
not manage it alone. Ask directly whether they are thinking about hurting
themselves — asking does not plant the idea, and it is often a relief to be
asked. Then help them talk to someone tonight.

## Last thing

You are going to say something imperfect. Say it anyway.

The people who get remembered are not the ones who found the right words. They
are the ones who kept turning up after everybody else stopped.

Moving on feels like losing them twice because, in a way, it is — the first
loss took them, and this second one feels like it is taking the version of you
that was still organised around missing them. That version has been doing a
kind of work: keeping the loss close, keeping them present through the pain of
it. Letting the pain ease can feel like letting go of the last thing still
holding them here.

This is one of the more counterintuitive things about grief. The relief you
are supposed to feel as things get easier sometimes arrives instead as panic —
because if it stops hurting this much, what does that say about how much they
mattered?

## "Moving on" was always the wrong phrase

Part of the problem is the phrase itself. It implies a place you leave behind —
them — for a place you arrive at, without them. No wonder it feels like a
betrayal; described that way, it would be one.

What actually happens, for almost everyone, is closer to *moving forward with*
than *moving on from*. The person does not get left at a point in the past you
walk away from. They come with you, differently than before — carried instead
of constantly, present instead of consuming — but still there. If the language
you are using for this makes it sound like abandonment, it is worth finding
different language, because the feeling itself is not actually asking you to
abandon anyone.

## The pain was never the proof

Somewhere along the way, many people quietly start using the intensity of their
pain as the measure of how much they loved someone. By that logic, less pain
means less love, and healing starts to feel dangerous rather than good.

But pain was never a very accurate ruler for love. It measures how raw the loss
still is, not how much the person meant. As the rawness fades — which it does,
unevenly, on no fixed schedule — the love underneath it does not fade at the
same rate, or necessarily at all. It just stops being the only thing you feel.

## What actually helps

**Retire the phrase "moving on," even just for yourself.** Try "moving forward
with them" or nothing at all. Small as it sounds, the words you use to describe
what is happening shape how threatening it feels.

**Let a good day be about the day, not a referendum on the relationship.** One
easier afternoon does not put anything on trial. It does not need defending.

**Keep a place for them in whatever comes next.** People do this in enormously
different ways — a photo that stays out, a habit that continues, a name still
mentioned in ordinary conversation. Healing does not require erasing the
evidence that they existed and mattered.

**Notice when panic, not sadness, is what's actually happening.** If an easier
stretch triggers something closer to fear than grief, that is worth naming
specifically. It is usually the fear of forgetting, not actual forgetting,
because the people who are truly forgetting someone do not spend energy being
afraid of it.

## One more thing

You are not going to lose them twice. The first loss was real and it was
theirs — it happened to them, and to you. This second fear is not another loss
at all. It is what healing feels like from the inside, before you have had time
to see that carrying someone more lightly is not the same as putting them down.

If tonight the fear of forgetting is louder than the grief itself, it can help
to say their name somewhere, out loud or in writing, just to prove to yourself
that saying it does not make it less true. That is part of what this place is
for.

title: Why Does Everyone Else Seem Fine?

description: When your grief feels unbearable and everyone around you looks like they're managing, here's what you're actually seeing — and not seeing.
---

Everyone else looks fine because fine is what people show, not what they carry.
You are comparing your inside to their outside, and that comparison will lose
every time, because it is not a fair one.

You have full access to your own grief — the 2am version, the version at your
desk pretending to read an email, the version that shows up in a supermarket
aisle for no reason. You have almost none of that access to anyone else's. What
you see is the version they have already arranged for other people to see.

## Managing is not the same as fine

Most people at work, in line, at the family dinner, are running a version of
themselves that has been edited for public use — not out of dishonesty, but
because that is what functioning in front of other people requires. Underneath
it, the same person might be exactly where you are: distracted, raw, holding it
together by a thread that snaps the moment they get to their car.

You would not know, because that is the whole point of the edit. So "everyone
seems fine" is not really a description of everyone. It is a description of
what people let you see.

## Grief does not look like grief, most of the time

There is also a mismatch between what grief actually looks like day to day and
what we expect it to look like. It is not usually visible tears in public. Most
of the time it is smaller and stranger: forgetting words mid-sentence, being
short with someone over nothing, feeling nothing at all when you expected to
feel something. None of that reads as *grieving* from the outside. It just
reads as a person having an ordinary day, badly.

So when you scan a room for who else looks like you feel, you are looking for
the wrong signal. Almost nobody's face says it. That does not mean nobody else
is carrying it.

## The specific loneliness of feeling like the only one

This comparison does real damage beyond the sadness itself — it adds a second,
quieter belief: *something is wrong with me, specifically, for taking this so
hard.* That belief is not supported by anything except an incomplete sample.
You are one person with total visibility into one grief. Everyone else is a
stranger with none.

## What actually helps

**Ask one person directly, if you can.** Not "how are you" — the answer to that
is built for public use too. Something closer to "is this as hard for you as it
is for me" tends to get an honest answer, and it is often surprising.

**Stop grading yourself against a room you cannot see into.** You do not know
what anyone else is carrying today, this year, or ever. The grading is happening
against imaginary data.

**Let your own face be the exception, sometimes.** You do not owe anyone the
edited version all the time. Letting one person see the real one is not a
failure to cope — it is one of the only ways anyone finds out they are not as
alone as the room made them feel.

## One more thing

If everyone truly does seem unbothered while you are struggling, it is worth
asking a doctor or someone who knows you well whether what you are carrying has
become heavier than it should be to carry alone — that is a real possibility
too, and it is not something to wait out quietly. But for most people, most of
the time, the answer is simpler and less lonely than it feels: the room is not
actually fine. It is just good at looking like it.

Sometimes it helps to say the real thing somewhere nobody is performing back at
you. That is what this place is for.
